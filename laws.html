Legal Framework

Pennsylvania Cannabis Law & Regulation

Pennsylvania operates two parallel regulatory systems for cannabis. Understanding both is essential for any research, education, or commercial initiative.

Overview: Two Parallel Systems

Pennsylvania distinguishes between two regulatory frameworks depending on THC content and intended use. Each is administered by a different state agency with separate permitting, compliance, and enforcement structures.

Key Distinction

Hemp (cannabis with ≤0.3% THC by dry weight) is regulated by the PA Department of Agriculture (PDA) under the state's Hemp Program, authorized by the 2018 Farm Bill (7 U.S.C. §1639o) and enabled by the PA Controlled Plants and Noxious Weeds Act (3 Pa.C.S. §701 et seq.).

Medical Marijuana (cannabis with >0.3% THC) is regulated by the PA Department of Health (DOH) under the Medical Marijuana Act of 2016 (35 P.S. §10231.101 et seq.).

1. The Hemp Program (PA Department of Agriculture)

The hemp program is governed by the PA Industrial Hemp Act (3 Pa.C.S. §701), which incorporates the federal definition of hemp under the 2018 Farm Bill. The PDA issues permits under the PA Hemp General Permit through its Bureau of Plant Industry. The program is significantly more accessible for new entrants than the medical marijuana program.

2026 Permit Data

As of the 2026 growing season, the PDA has issued:

  • 115 hemp growing permits (including 11 research permits)
  • 32 hemp processing permits (including 1 research permit)

Applications for new and renewal permits for 2027 are accepted beginning October 1st and throughout the year. There is no cap — applications are ongoing. See the PDA Hemp Program page for current forms and the PA Hemp Permit Map and Database.

Permit Requirements

  • Application fee: Varies by permit type; check current PDA fee schedule
  • Background check required for all applicants
  • Must provide GPS coordinates of all growing/handling locations
  • Must use approved certified seed or approved genetics
  • Compliance testing for THC levels required before harvest
  • Annual reporting on acreage, yield, and disposal

THC Compliance Testing (Current Standard)

Crops must test ≤0.3% Delta-9 THC by dry weight under the current federal definition (7 U.S.C. §1639o, pre-H.R. 5371). Any crop exceeding this threshold is subject to a destruction order. Important: This standard changes to a total THC standard (including THCA) effective November 12, 2026 — see Section 7 below for details.

Location Restrictions

  • Hemp cultivation sites cannot be within 3 miles of an existing medical marijuana grower/processor without special approval
  • Distance restrictions from schools and residential areas may apply depending on local zoning

Key Advantage for Education

Hemp permits are ideal for educational greenhouse operations, clone production, breeding research, and student training because they avoid the stringent security and capital requirements of the medical program. Low-THC varieties can be handled safely in academic settings.

2. The Medical Marijuana Program (PA Department of Health)

Enacted by the Medical Marijuana Act (Act 16 of 2016), Pennsylvania's program is one of the most tightly regulated in the country. It includes three permit types:

Application Windows

The DOH has run multiple application phases (most recently Phase V in 2021 and Phase VI in 2024). As of mid-2026, no new application windows are open for grower/processor, dispensary, or Clinical Registrant permits. New entry is only possible through acquisition or transfer of existing permits under Act 63 rules.

3. Clinical Registrants (CRs)

Clinical Registrants are a unique PA category — entities that hold both a grower/processor permit and a dispensary permit, coupled with an Academic Clinical Research Center (ACRC) partnership to conduct medical marijuana research.

Statutory cap: 10 CRs. All slots are currently filled.

Perks: Each CR can operate up to 6 dispensary locations (with limits per region/county).

Research requirement: CRs must actively conduct research with their partnered ACRC (a medical school at a Pennsylvania university).

See the full Clinical Registrants page for profiles of all 10 CRs and their ACRC partners.

4. Act 63 (2024) — Recent Changes

Pennsylvania's Act 63 updated several aspects of the medical marijuana program:

  • Expanded patient access and caregiver provisions
  • Modified permit transfer and acquisition rules
  • Created pathways for existing permit holders to modify operations
  • Did not increase the CR cap of 10

5. Local Zoning Considerations

In Bucks County / Newtown / Doylestown area (relevant to a proposed greenhouse operation):

  • Agricultural/horticultural uses (including commercial nurseries and greenhouses) are generally permitted or conditionally permitted
  • Medical marijuana grower/processor uses require specific local approvals in many township ordinances
  • Always verify exact zoning with the relevant township for any proposed grow site

6. Social Equity & Diversity Requirements

PA's medical marijuana program requires diversity plans from applicants. Social-impact hiring (people with disabilities, workforce re-entry) strengthens applications and is explicitly aligned with:

  • PA Employment First policy
  • Office of Vocational Rehabilitation (OVR) programs
  • InVEST project initiatives
  • PA Center for Plant Excellence workforce goals

7. Federal Hemp Definition Change (H.R. 5371, effective Nov 12, 2026)

The most significant change to federal hemp policy since the 2018 Farm Bill was enacted on November 12, 2025, as part of the Continuing Appropriations, Agriculture, Legislative Branch, Military Construction and Veterans Affairs, and Extensions Act, 2026 (H.R. 5371, P.L. 119-37). The hemp provisions are at Division B, §781, amending the definition of hemp in 7 U.S.C. §1639o. These changes take effect November 12, 2026 — one year after enactment.

What Changes

  • Total THC standard: Hemp's THC limit shifts from Delta-9-only (0.3% by dry weight) to total THC (including THCA, Delta-8, Delta-10, and any other cannabinoids with similar effects as determined by HHS). After decarboxylation, total THC must be ≤0.3% on a dry weight basis.
  • 0.4 mg per-container cap: Final hemp-derived cannabinoid products (any product intended for human or animal consumption) are limited to 0.4 milligrams of total THC per container (the innermost retail packaging). This is a micro-dose threshold — virtually every current intoxicating product (5–50 mg edibles, vapes, tinctures, THCA flower) exceeds it by orders of magnitude.
  • Synthetic/semisynthetic exclusion: Cannabinoids that are not naturally produced by the living Cannabis sativa L. plant, or that are produced via synthesis, isomerization, or chemical conversion outside the plant, are excluded from the hemp definition entirely. This captures the entire CBD-to-Delta-8 conversion market.
  • Industrial hemp preserved: Fiber, grain, oil, seed cake, and microgreens from non-cannabinoid hemp remain fully protected — unaffected by the new rules.

FDA Guidance (Deadline Feb 10, 2026)

H.R. 5371 directed the FDA to publish by February 10, 2026: (1) a list of naturally occurring cannabinoids, (2) a list of THC-class cannabinoids, (3) a list of cannabinoids with THC-like effects, and (4) container definition guidance. These lists serve as the operational reference for determining which cannabinoids count toward the total THC limit.

Impact on PA Hemp Growers & Processors

  • Growers: Total THC testing will cause more crops to test "hot" — especially high-THCA hemp flower varieties that pass the Delta-9-only test but exceed 0.3% total THC. Growers should select genetics with naturally low total THC.
  • Processors: The 0.4 mg per-container cap eliminates most current intoxicating hemp product lines. Processors focused on non-intoxicating products (topical CBD, fiber, grain, animal feed, industrial applications) are unaffected.
  • Retailers (Farm Bill stores): THCA flower, Delta-8 gummies/vapes/carts, and high-potency tinctures will lose federal hemp status after Nov 12, 2026. Retailers must either reformulate to sub-0.4 mg levels or pivot to non-intoxicating product lines.

8. SB 49 — Pennsylvania's (Failed) Response

Senate Bill 49 (Laughlin, 2025-2026 session) was the primary PA legislative response to the federal hemp changes. Key provisions included:

  • Establishing a PA Cannabis Control Board (CCB) to oversee both medical marijuana and hemp-derived products under one regulatory structure
  • Aligning PA's hemp definition with the new federal total THC standard and 0.4 mg per-container cap
  • Banning synthetic cannabinoids and intoxicating hemp products not meeting the new standards
  • Transferring medical marijuana oversight from DOH to the CCB
  • Setting a $10,000 fine for illegal intoxicating hemp product sales

Status: Failed (June 10, 2026)

SB 49 was defeated in the Senate by a 27-23 vote. Bipartisan opposition emerged after 7 of 9 Democratic co-sponsors withdrew support. Critics argued the bill was unnecessary, that PA should pursue adult-use legalization instead, and that the hemp provisions would destroy legitimate businesses without providing a regulatory framework for compliance.

Consequences of SB 49's Failure

  • Regulatory gap: After November 12, 2026, intoxicating hemp products will be federally illegal (Schedule I under the Controlled Substances Act) but remain potentially legal under PA's Industrial Hemp Act (3 Pa.C.S. §701). This creates enforcement uncertainty.
  • No Cannabis Control Board: DOH continues to oversee medical marijuana. No unified regulatory structure exists.
  • Prosecutorial discretion: Enforcement of the federal definition change in PA will depend on DEA/FDA resources and local U.S. Attorney priorities. PA county DAs have varied positions on hemp-derived intoxicants.
  • Ongoing legislative options: Laughlin or other sponsors may reintroduce similar legislation in future sessions. The federal change applies in PA regardless of state action.

10. Compliance Essentials

Any cannabis operation in Pennsylvania must prioritize:

  • Seed-to-sale tracking (mandatory for medical; recommended for hemp)
  • Third-party lab testing for potency, contaminants, and pesticides
  • Security (video surveillance, access control, inventory controls)
  • Traceability — documented chain of custody for all plant material
  • Stay current on federal hemp definition changes — the November 2026 effective date will affect THC compliance standards, product formulations, and interstate commerce protections
